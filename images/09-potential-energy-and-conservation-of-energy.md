# Figures — Chapter 9 — Potential Energy and Conservation of Energy

## Figure 9.1: Roller Coaster Energy Exchange

[FIGURE: A side-view sketch of a roller coaster track showing three hills of decreasing height: 50m, 40m, and 30m. At the crest of each hill, two vertical bars: a red bar (representing kinetic energy) and a blue bar (representing potential energy). At the bottom between hills, the bars are inverted: the red bar is tall, the blue bar is short. A horizontal black line connects the tops of all bars, showing constant total energy (in the frictionless case). A second, slightly lower curved line shows the actual energy with friction, declining over the ride.]

**Caption:** In a frictionless system, the sum of kinetic and potential energy stays constant (black line). Friction slowly drains the total (curved line below). At each point, you can read off the kinetic energy (red) and potential energy (blue) from the height of each bar. The rolling motion from crest to valley to crest is a continuous exchange between forms of the same total.

## Figure 9.2: Gravitational Potential Energy — Linear in Height

[FIGURE: A Cartesian plot with height y (in meters) on the horizontal axis (0 to 10 m) and potential energy U(y) (in joules) on the vertical axis (0 to 1000 J). A straight line passes through the origin with positive slope, labeled U(y) = mgy. (Parameters: m = 10 kg, g = 10 m/s².) A horizontal line at U = 500 J intersects the curve at y = 5 m, marking the turning point for a particle with E = 500 J. The region to the left of this intersection is shaded, representing allowed motion; the region to the right (where U > E) is unshaded, representing forbidden motion.]

**Caption:** Gravitational potential energy grows linearly with height. For a given total energy E, the particle can only reach a maximum height y_max = E/(mg). Above that height, potential energy exceeds total energy, making kinetic energy negative — impossible.

## Figure 9.3: Elastic Potential Energy — Quadratic in Displacement

[FIGURE: A Cartesian plot with displacement x (in meters) on the horizontal axis from -1 to +1 and potential energy U(x) (in joules) on the vertical axis from 0 to 5 J. A parabola opens upward, labeled U(x) = (1/2)kx², with minimum at x = 0 and k = 10 N/m. A horizontal line at U = 2 J intersects the parabola at two points: x ≈ -0.63 m and x ≈ +0.63 m. These turning points are marked with vertical dashed lines. The region between the turning points is shaded, representing allowed motion. The equilibrium at x = 0 is marked with a filled dot.]

**Caption:** Elastic potential energy is quadratic: it grows as the square of displacement. For a given energy E, the particle oscillates between two symmetric turning points. The equilibrium at x = 0 (the minimum) is stable — any small displacement triggers a restoring force.

## Figure 9.4: Potential Energy Diagram — Stability and Equilibrium

[FIGURE: A smooth curve showing potential energy U(x) on the vertical axis and position x on the horizontal axis. The curve has two local minima (valleys) separated by a local maximum (hilltop) in the middle. The left minimum is deeper than the right. Three filled dots mark the three equilibrium points: two at the minima (labeled "stable") and one at the maximum (labeled "unstable"). A horizontal line at energy E_1 intersects the curve in four regions, creating two separate allowed bands (shaded). A second line at E_2 (higher) intersects at two points and permits motion over the entire range.]

**Caption:** Minima of potential energy are stable equilibrium points (the force pushes back). Maxima are unstable (the force pushes away). The allowed regions for motion depend on the total energy E: a low-energy particle gets trapped in one well; a high-energy particle can escape and roam the whole landscape.

## Figure 9.5: Double-Well Potential and Confined Motion

[FIGURE: A double-well potential U(x) = 2(x⁴ - x²) plotted from x = -1.5 to +1.5 meters, with U ranging from -0.6 to +1 J. The curve shows two symmetric minima (at x ≈ ±0.707 m, depth U ≈ -0.5 J) separated by a central maximum (at x = 0, height U = 0). A horizontal line at E = -0.25 J sits between the central maximum and the minima, creating two separate allowed regions (shaded gray): one on the left side and one on the right, roughly from x = -0.92 to -0.38 and from x = 0.38 to 0.92. A second line at E = 0.5 J sits above the central maximum, permitting motion in the outer regions where x < -1 or x > 1.]

**Caption:** A particle with low energy (E = -0.25 J) is confined to one well and cannot cross to the other. Increasing the energy (E = 0.5 J) allows it to escape and roam the outer landscape. This is why potential barriers matter in chemistry and quantum mechanics: the energy determines what regions are reachable.

## Figure 9.6: Pendulum — Height and Angle

[FIGURE: A side view of a pendulum: a fixed pivot point at the top, a string of length L descending, and a bob at the end. The string is shown at three positions: (a) at maximum displacement to the left, making an angle θ with the vertical; (b) at the lowest point (vertical); and (c) at maximum displacement to the right, making angle θ with the vertical. For position (a), a vertical dashed line shows the height h = L(1 - cos θ) above the lowest point. The bob's speed is zero at (a) and (c), and maximum at (b). Energy bars appear at each position: at (a) and (c), the U bar is tall and the K bar is empty; at (b), the U bar is zero and the K bar is full.]

**Caption:** A pendulum oscillates between two turning points at the same height h. At the extremes, all energy is potential; at the bottom, all is kinetic. The height difference h = L(1 - cos θ) determines the maximum speed: v_max = √(2gh).

## Figure 9.7: Spring-Mass System at Four Stages

[FIGURE: Four vertical diagrams, each showing a spring hanging from a fixed support, with a mass at the end. (1) Spring at natural length, mass just touching, K = 0, U_grav = 0, U_spring = 0, E = 0. (2) Spring stretched slightly, mass moving downward, K > 0, U_grav < 0, U_spring > 0, total E = 0. (3) Spring maximally stretched (lowest point), mass momentarily at rest, K = 0, U_grav at minimum (most negative), U_spring at maximum, E = 0. (4) Spring returning to natural length, mass moving upward, K > 0, U_grav > 0, U_spring = 0, E = 0. An energy bar chart to the side shows how the three forms trade places while E stays constant.]

**Caption:** A mass hanging from a spring trades gravitational potential energy for elastic potential energy and kinetic energy. At any moment, the sum K + U_grav + U_spring remains constant. The motion is a complex dance of three energy forms, yet their total never changes.

## Figure 9.8: Helicopter Panel Falling — Energy Dissipation

[FIGURE: A graph with time (in seconds) on the horizontal axis and total mechanical energy (in kilojoules) on the vertical axis. A horizontal dashed line at E = 147 kJ represents the initial mechanical energy (all gravitational potential energy at height 1000 m). A curve descends steeply at first, then less steeply, reaching a final value of approximately 15 kJ (the kinetic energy at impact, 45 m/s). The shaded region between the dashed line and the curve represents the energy dissipated by air drag — the "missing" mechanical energy that has become heat. At the final point, a label shows: "E_dissipated ≈ 130 kJ" and "All transferred to heat in the air."]

**Caption:** A falling object loses mechanical energy continuously to air drag. The panel's initial potential energy (147 kJ) is only partly recovered as kinetic energy at impact (15 kJ); the rest (130 kJ) heats the air. This is why parachutes work: they increase drag, converting kinetic energy to heat (and slowing the descent) rather than letting it become impact velocity.

## Figure 9.9: Turning Points and Allowed Regions on a PE Diagram

[FIGURE: A potential energy curve U(x) resembling a parabola or a general curve with a minimum. The x-axis spans from -3 to 3 meters, the U-axis from 0 to 6 joules. The curve's minimum is at x = 0, U = 0.5 J. Two horizontal lines: one at E_1 = 1.5 J (low energy) and one at E_2 = 3 J (higher energy). For E_1, the line intersects the curve at two points (x ≈ -1.2 and x ≈ +1.2), defining a confined region where motion is allowed (shaded). **Caption:** The horizontal energy line divides the diagram into allowed (shaded) and forbidden (white) regions. Turning points (where K = 0) occur where the energy line intersects the potential curve. Higher total energy permits wider regions of motion.

## Figure 9.10: Stable vs. Unstable Equilibrium

[FIGURE: Two side-by-side potential curves. (Left) A curve with a local minimum (valley): the second derivative d²U/dx² > 0. At the minimum, three small vectors point outward (left and right from the equilibrium), showing the restoring force direction. A label says "Stable: force points back to equilibrium." (Right) A curve with a local maximum (hilltop): d²U/dx² < 0. At the maximum, three small vectors point outward and away from equilibrium, showing the force pushes the particle further away. A label says "Unstable: force points away from equilibrium." Both curves have a filled dot marking the equilibrium point.]

**Caption:** At a stable equilibrium (minimum), the slope is zero and the second derivative is positive. A displaced particle experiences a restoring force. At an unstable equilibrium (maximum), the slope is still zero but the second derivative is negative. A displaced particle experiences a force pushing it further away — like a pencil balanced on its tip.

## Figure 9.11: Energy Conservation Across a Multi-Stage Path

[FIGURE: A roller coaster-like track with multiple hills and valleys. Five points are labeled A, B, C, D, E, each at different heights. At each point, an energy bar chart (like a stacked bar) shows the breakdown of total energy E into K (red) and U (blue). The heights of the bar stacks are identical at all five points, reinforcing that E = K + U is constant throughout (assuming no friction). The kinetic energy is largest at the valleys (B and D) and smallest at the peaks (A, C, E). The potential energy is largest at the peaks and smallest at the valleys.]

**Caption:** Tracking energy through a complex path: at each stage, calculate K and U. If you've done it right, the sum E = K + U will be the same everywhere. Differences (losses) indicate non-conservative forces like friction at work.

## Figure 9.12: The Spring Force from the Potential Curve

[FIGURE: Two plots stacked vertically. (Top) Potential energy U(x) = (1/2)kx² plotted from x = -2 to +2 m. The parabola is symmetric about x = 0. (Bottom) Force F(x) = -dU/dx = -kx plotted on the same x-range. This is a straight line passing through the origin, positive for x < 0 (force pushes right, back toward equilibrium) and negative for x > 0 (force pushes left, back toward equilibrium). At several x-values, small arrows on the U curve at the top are matched with the F curve at the bottom, showing the relationship: where the U curve slopes down (negative dU/dx), the force F is positive (upward arrow), and vice versa.]

**Caption:** Force and potential energy are related by F = -dU/dx. The slope of the U curve (the steepness) determines the strength of the force. At a minimum (zero slope), the force is zero — the equilibrium point. The pairing between the two curves (U above, F below) shows how the derivative relationship works graphically.
