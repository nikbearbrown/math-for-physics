# Images — Chapter 11, Fixed-Axis Rotation

Manifest of figures, diagrams, and visual aids needed for the chapter.

## Figure 1 — Angular position on a circle

**Type:** Diagram (SVG recommended).

**Description:** A circle with center marked. A radius drawn from center to a point on the circumference. An arc labeled $s$ between a reference direction (horizontal) and the radius. The angle $\theta$ marked between the reference and the radius. Labels: $\theta$ (angle in radians), $s$ (arc length), $r$ (radius).

**Purpose:** Introduce the definition $\theta = s/r$. Show that angle is arc length divided by radius.

**Caption:** *The angle θ in radians is the ratio of arc length to radius. One radian is the angle subtended by an arc equal in length to the radius.*

**Placement:** Concept 1, after the opening discussion of radians.

## Figure 2 — Tangential and centripetal acceleration

**Type:** Diagram (vector arrows on a circle).

**Description:** A circle with a point marked on the rim. Velocity vector drawn tangent to the circle at that point (arrow pointing along the circle). Centripetal acceleration vector drawn pointing toward the center (arrow from point toward center). Tangential acceleration vector drawn along the tangent direction (arrow in direction of motion if speeding up, opposite if slowing). Labels: $\vec{v}_t$, $\vec{a}_c$, $\vec{a}_t$.

**Purpose:** Visually separate the two accelerations in circular motion. Show they are perpendicular.

**Caption:** *In circular motion, centripetal acceleration (toward the center) changes the direction of velocity, while tangential acceleration (along the path) changes the magnitude. The two are always perpendicular.*

**Placement:** Concept 1, after discussion of $a_c = r\omega^2$ and $a_t = r\alpha$.

## Figure 3 — Moment of inertia comparison

**Type:** Diagram (three objects: hoop, disk, rod).

**Description:** Three objects of equal mass $M$ and equal outer radius $R$ (or length $L$ for the rod):
1. A thin hoop (ring) with all mass at radius $R$.
2. A solid disk with mass distributed from center to edge.
3. A uniform rod extending from the axis, with mass spread along its length.

Beneath each: the formula for $I$ ($I = MR^2$, $I = \frac{1}{2}MR^2$, $I = \frac{1}{3}ML^2$). A bar chart showing relative magnitudes.

**Purpose:** Show visually that distribution of mass matters. Hoop has the largest moment of inertia; disk is intermediate; rod is smallest (if rotating about one end).

**Caption:** *Objects of the same mass and size can have very different moments of inertia depending on how the mass is distributed. The hoop concentrates mass at the edge; the disk spreads it; the rod has mass all along its length.*

**Placement:** Concept 2, after calculation of $I$ for standard shapes.

## Figure 4 — The parallel-axis theorem

**Type:** Diagram (side view of a disk or rod).

**Description:** A thin disk or rod shown in side view. Center of mass marked with a black dot. Axis through center of mass drawn as a vertical line (perpendicular to page or parallel to page as appropriate). A second axis drawn parallel to the first, displaced horizontally by distance $d$. Labels: $I_{cm}$ (moment about center axis), $d$ (perpendicular distance), $I = I_{cm} + Md^2$ (moment about displaced axis).

**Purpose:** Visually represent the parallel-axis theorem. Show how shifting the axis by distance $d$ increases the moment of inertia.

**Caption:** *The parallel-axis theorem: the moment of inertia about any axis is the moment about a parallel axis through the center of mass, plus $Md^2$ where $d$ is the perpendicular distance between axes.*

**Placement:** Concept 2, after stating the theorem.

## Figure 5 — Torque on a door (or wheel)

**Type:** Diagram (top-down view of a disk with a force applied).

**Description:** A circle (disk viewed from above) with center marked. An axis of rotation drawn through the center. A radius drawn from center outward. A force vector drawn at the end of the radius, at angle $\theta$ to the radius. Labels: $\vec{F}$, $r$, $\theta$ (angle between $\vec{r}$ and $\vec{F}$), $\tau = rF\sin\theta$.

**Optional variant:** Show the door example explicitly — a rectangle (door) with a hinge axis on the left side, a force applied at the handle on the right, showing how the torque is larger when the force is far from the hinge.

**Purpose:** Illustrate the definition of torque and how it depends on the distance from the axis and the angle of the applied force.

**Caption:** *Torque is the rotational effect of a force. It depends on how far the force is applied from the axis (the moment arm $r$), the magnitude of the force $F$, and the angle θ between the force and the radius. Only the perpendicular component of the force contributes to rotation.*

**Placement:** Concept 3, after the definition of torque.

## Figure 6 — Centrifuge rotor (medical)

**Type:** Photograph or detailed diagram.

**Description:** A medical centrifuge rotor shown from above or in side view. Small sample vials visible in the rotor head, arranged in a circle. Labels: rotor radius, sample position, direction of rotation, g-force marking (showing 6,000× or 10,000×).

**Purpose:** Ground the chapter's opening in a real device. Show students what the abstract physics looks like in practice.

**Caption:** *A laboratory centrifuge spins at 10,000–15,000 rpm, creating centrifugal accelerations of thousands of g. Blood samples in the rotor are pushed outward, causing cells to separate from plasma.*

**Placement:** Chapter opening or Concept 1, to reinforce the real-world application.

## Figure 7 — Rolling without slipping

**Type:** Diagram (wheel rolling down an incline or on flat ground).

**Description:** A wheel (circle) shown in side view, partially on an inclined plane (or flat ground). The contact point with the ground marked. Center of mass marked. A velocity vector at the center pointing down the slope. An angular velocity vector (using right-hand rule, curl fingers in direction of rotation, thumb points out of page or along the axis). Label indicating: $v_{cm} = R\omega$ (the no-slip condition).

**Purpose:** Visually show the relationship between linear and angular motion in rolling. Emphasize the contact point has zero velocity.

**Caption:** *In rolling without slipping, the center of mass moves at speed $v_{cm} = R\omega$. The contact point with the ground instantaneously has zero velocity — this is what "no slip" means.*

**Placement:** Concept 3 or Integration, when discussing rolling motion.

## Figure 8 — Race of sphere vs. shell down an incline

**Type:** Diagram (two objects rolling down a slope, snapshots at different times).

**Description:** An inclined plane with two objects (a solid sphere and a hollow spherical shell) starting from rest at the top. Three or four snapshots showing the objects at different times, with the solid sphere ahead of the shell. Labels showing which is which, and the moment of inertia formula for each.

**Purpose:** Visually show that moment of inertia affects the outcome of a rolling race. The denser object (smaller $I/MR^2$) is faster.

**Caption:** *A solid sphere beats a hollow shell in a race down an incline, even though they have the same mass and radius. The difference is in how the mass is distributed — the moment of inertia.*

**Placement:** Concept 3 (worked example on rolling objects).

## Figure 9 — Angular acceleration vs. centripetal acceleration (graph)

**Type:** Graph (two curves).

**Description:** Time on the x-axis. Acceleration magnitude on the y-axis. For a disk speeding up ($\alpha = $ constant, $\omega$ increasing with time):
- Centripetal acceleration $a_c = r\omega^2$ shown as a curve increasing as $\omega^2$.
- Tangential acceleration $a_t = r\alpha$ shown as a horizontal line (constant).

Curves intersect at some time, and $a_c$ dominates afterward.

**Purpose:** Show that at the start of rotation, tangential acceleration dominates; as speed increases, centripetal acceleration becomes large and eventually dominates.

**Caption:** *For a disk starting from rest with constant angular acceleration, tangential acceleration $a_t = r\alpha$ is constant, while centripetal acceleration $a_c = r\omega^2$ grows with the square of time. At high speeds, centripetal acceleration dominates.*

**Placement:** Concept 1 or as a wrap-up after discussing both accelerations.

## Figure 10 — Vector cross product and torque direction

**Type:** Diagram (3D perspective or multiple views).

**Description:** A coordinate system (x, y, z axes shown). A position vector $\vec{r}$ drawn from the origin. A force vector $\vec{F}$ drawn at the tip of $\vec{r}$. The cross product $\vec{\tau} = \vec{r} \times \vec{F}$ shown using the right-hand rule: fingers curl from $\vec{r}$ to $\vec{F}$, thumb points along $\vec{\tau}$ (out of the page or in some 3D direction).

**Purpose:** Introduce the vector form of torque and the right-hand rule.

**Caption:** *Torque as a vector is $\vec{\tau} = \vec{r} \times \vec{F}$. The direction is given by the right-hand rule: curl your fingers from $\vec{r}$ toward $\vec{F}$, and your thumb points along the torque vector.*

**Placement:** Concept 3 (optional, depending on depth of vector treatment).

## Figure 11 — Centrifuge operation (schematic flow)

**Type:** Diagram or infographic (blood separation process).

**Description:** A vial of blood shown at rest (mixed) and after centrifugation (separated into layers). Top layer: plasma (yellowish). Middle layer: white blood cells (thin buffy coat). Bottom layer: red blood cells (dark red). Arrows showing the outward centrifugal acceleration during spinning.

**Purpose:** Show the practical outcome of the physics: separation of blood components.

**Caption:** *Centrifugation at high speed applies a strong outward acceleration to the sample. Denser blood cells move outward faster, separating from the lighter plasma. The result is used to calculate the hematocrit, a measure of red blood cell concentration.*

**Placement:** Chapter opening or Integration section.

## Production notes

- All diagrams should use consistent color: blue for velocity vectors, red for force/torque vectors, black for axes and radii.
- Arrows should be large and clear, with labels placed outside the arrows.
- Use SI units throughout (m, s, rad, N, N·m, kg·m²).
- Avoid clutter: each figure should make one point clearly.
- Include a 1:1 scale reference (e.g., "1 cm = 10 m on the page") if actual proportions matter.
