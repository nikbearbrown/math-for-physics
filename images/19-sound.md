## Concept 1: Sound as Longitudinal Pressure Wave

### [FIGURE 1.1] Longitudinal Wave in Air: Speaker Oscillation to Pressure Wave
**Source:** OpenStax University Physics 17.01

Diagram showing:
- Speaker cone oscillating back and forth
- Air molecules bunched up (compressions, high pressure, blue) in front of speaker when cone moves forward
- Air molecules spread out (rarefactions, low pressure, red) behind speaker
- Successive compressions moving outward as wavefronts
- Pressure vs. distance graph showing sinusoidal variation with wavelength marked
- Displacement vs. distance graph showing oscillation of molecules

**Purpose:** Illustrate the origin of sound as a disturbance of medium molecules; show both pressure and displacement models simultaneously; clarify that oscillation is parallel to propagation direction (longitudinal).

**Callouts in chapter:** Used in opening discussion of "physical phenomenon of sound." Referenced to motivate the dual pressure/displacement models.

## Concept 2: Intensity and Decibels

### [FIGURE 2.1] Inverse Square Law: Intensity vs. Distance
**Source:** Geometric derivation; standard acoustics diagram

Diagram showing:
- Point source at center
- Spherical wavefront at radius $r_1$ (blue circle) with surface area $4\pi r_1^2$
- Same wavefront at radius $r_2 = 2r_1$ (larger blue circle) with surface area $4\pi r_2^2$
- Power $P$ shown as arrows radiating outward
- Label: "Same power spread over 4× larger area → 1/4 intensity"
- Formula overlay: $I_2 = I_1 (r_1/r_2)^2$

**Purpose:** Visually derive the inverse square law without equations; explain why sound gets quieter with distance; set up the decibel reference to threshold-of-hearing at a fixed distance.

**Callouts:** Used in opening of Concept 2 section.

## Concept 3: Doppler Effect and Shock Waves

### [FIGURE 3.1] Doppler Effect: Stationary vs. Moving Source
**Source:** OpenStax figure 17.26

Diagram showing two cases side by side:

**Case A (Stationary source):**
- Point source at center
- Concentric circles (wavefronts) equally spaced
- Both left and right observers equidistant from source
- Label: "Same wavelength heard in both directions"

**Case B (Moving source approaching to the right):**
- Point source offset to the left, moving right (velocity arrow shown)
- Wavefronts bunched up on the right side of source (shorter wavelength)
- Wavefronts spread out on the left side (longer wavelength)
- Observer on right (ahead of motion): bunched wavefronts
- Observer on left (behind motion): spread wavefronts
- Label: "Shorter wavelength → higher frequency (right); longer wavelength → lower frequency (left)"

**Purpose:** Visualize the physical cause of Doppler shift (bunching and spreading of wavefronts); make clear that the effect is direction-dependent; prepare for the asymmetry between source motion and observer motion.

**Callouts:** Used to introduce Concept 3; referenced when deriving Doppler formulas.

---

### [FIGURE 3.2] Shock Wave: Mach Cone from Supersonic Object
**Source:** OpenStax figure 17.33

Diagram showing:
- Aircraft moving left to right at speed $v_s > v$ (sound speed)
- Wavefront generated at time $t=0$ has expanded to radius $vt$
- Aircraft has moved distance $v_s t$ to the right
- Shock wave cone shown as tangent lines from aircraft to wavefront circle
- Cone angle $\theta$ labeled with $\sin \theta = vt / (v_s t) = v/v_s = 1/M$
- Mach number $M = v_s/v$ labeled
- Annotation: "Sound cannot escape the shock cone; constructive interference inside creates extreme pressure"

**Purpose:** Show the geometric origin of the shock wave cone angle; explain how $\sin \theta = 1/M$ arises from simple geometry; transition from Doppler effect (subsonic) to shock waves (supersonic).

**Callouts:** Used to derive Mach cone angle formula; referenced when explaining sonic booms.

### [FIGURE 3.3] Sonic Boom: Shock Wave Sweeping Past Observer
**Source:** OpenStax or NASA diagram

Diagram showing:
- Aircraft at supersonic speed passing overhead at altitude $h$
- Shock wave cone trailing the aircraft (cone angle determined by Mach number)
- Observer on ground below the cone path
- Before aircraft arrival: observer hears nothing
- As shock wave sweeps past observer: sudden loud boom (intense pressure spike)
- After aircraft passes: observer may hear the aircraft itself (if it descends enough)
- Timeline annotation: "Boom occurs when shock wave reaches observer, not when aircraft crosses sound barrier"
- Distance annotation: $d = h / \tan \theta$ showing horizontal distance to boom at ground level

**Purpose:** Dispel the misconception that sonic booms occur once, when "breaking the sound barrier"; show that boom is a continuous phenomenon as the shock cone sweeps past; connect geometry to observed phenomenon.

**Callouts:** Used in "Shock Waves and Sonic Booms" section to clarify timing and causation.

### [FIGURE 3.4] Felix Baumgartner: Visible Shock Wave
**Source:** Red Bull Stratos official video frame (October 14, 2012)

High-altitude photograph showing:
- Felix Baumgartner in freefall at ~37 km altitude
- Visible cone-shaped cloud structure around his body
- Cloud forms because air temperature drops rapidly inside shock wave (adiabatic compression), then expands again, and water vapor condenses when temperature drops
- Speed: Mach 1.25 (~1,357 km/h or 377 m/s)
- Caption: "Shock wave visible due to cloud formation (pressure-induced temperature drop → condensation)"

**Purpose:** Anchor the abstract concept of shock waves in a real, visible, documented phenomenon; motivate the student with an extreme example of physics working at human scale.

**Callouts:** Referenced in chapter opening and again in Concept 3 to make shock waves concrete.

## Concept 4: Standing Waves and Resonance

### [FIGURE 4.1] Tube Closed at One End: Standing Wave Patterns
**Source:** OpenStax figure 17.27

Diagram showing multiple rows (modes):

**Fundamental (n=1):**
- Single node at closed end (left)
- Single antinode at open end (right)
- Wavelength: $\lambda_1 = 4L$
- Frequency: $f_1 = v/(4L)$

**First overtone (n=3):**
- Three nodes, two antinodes
- Wavelength: $\lambda_3 = 4L/3$
- Frequency: $f_3 = 3v/(4L)$

**Third overtone (n=5):**
- Five nodes, four antinodes
- Wavelength: $\lambda_5 = 4L/5$
- Frequency: $f_5 = 5v/(4L)$

**Color coding:** Red for antinodes (maximum displacement), blue for nodes (zero displacement); amplitude envelope shown as curved boundary.

**Purpose:** Show that only odd harmonics resonate in a tube closed at one end; visually derive the wavelength relationships; explain the restriction due to asymmetric boundary conditions.

**Callouts:** Embedded in Concept 4, "Resonance in a Tube Closed at One End."

---

### [FIGURE 4.2] Tube Open at Both Ends: Standing Wave Patterns
**Source:** OpenStax figure 17.28

Diagram showing multiple rows (modes), parallel structure to Figure 4.1:

**Fundamental (n=1):**
- Antinodes at both ends
- Single node at center
- Wavelength: $\lambda_1 = 2L$
- Frequency: $f_1 = v/(2L)$

**First overtone (n=2):**
- Antinodes at both ends
- Nodes at L/2 and ... (wait, n=2 has two segments)
- Wavelength: $\lambda_2 = L$
- Frequency: $f_2 = 2v/(2L)$

**Second overtone (n=3):**
- Wavelength: $\lambda_3 = 2L/3$
- Frequency: $f_3 = 3v/(2L)$

**Contrast with Figure 4.1:** All integer harmonics present (n=1,2,3,...) vs. only odd harmonics. Fundamental is higher frequency for same length (open tube has higher pitch than closed tube).

**Purpose:** Directly compare closed-one-end vs. open-both-ends; show how boundary symmetry determines harmonic content; visually explain why flutes and clarinets sound different.

**Callouts:** Embedded in Concept 4, "Resonance in a Tube Open at Both Ends."

### [FIGURE 4.3] Timbre: Harmonic Content of Different Instruments
**Source:** Conceptual diagram; could reference actual spectrum analyzer data

Diagram showing overlaid bar graphs (harmonic spectra) for three instruments:

**Flute (pure sine wave approximation):**
- Large fundamental ($f_1$)
- Very small or absent higher harmonics
- Appearance: tall single bar
- Sound: pure, simple, flute-like

**Clarinet (closed tube, odd harmonics):**
- Large fundamental ($f_1$)
- Significant odd harmonics ($f_3, f_5, f_7$)
- Even harmonics absent ($f_2, f_4, f_6$)
- Appearance: saw-tooth pattern
- Sound: warm, woody

**Trumpet (complex harmonic mix):**
- Large fundamental ($f_1$)
- Significant all harmonics ($f_2, f_3, f_4, \ldots$)
- Higher harmonics persist (richer texture)
- Appearance: densely populated bar graph
- Sound: bright, brassy

**X-axis:** Frequency (marked as 1× fundamental, 2× fundamental, 3× fundamental, etc.)
**Y-axis:** Amplitude (relative intensity of each harmonic)

**Purpose:** Show that timbre is quantifiable (harmonic content); explain why instruments sound different despite playing the same fundamental frequency; tie resonance physics to musical perception.

**Callouts:** Used in Concept 4 section on "Timbre: Why a Violin and a Piano Sound Different on the Same Note."

## Integration Diagrams

### [FIGURE 5.1] The Wave Equation as Universal Principle
**Source:** Conceptual synthesis

Diagram showing:
- Center: $\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial t^2} = v^2 \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2}$ (the wave equation)
- Radiating spokes labeled with different realizations:
  - **Mechanical waves:** displacement $u = s(x,t)$ (string, tube), $v = \sqrt{T/\mu}$ (tension/density) or $v = \sqrt{B/\rho}$ (bulk modulus/density)
  - **Sound:** pressure $u = \Delta P(x,t)$, $v = \sqrt{\gamma RT/M}$ (gas) or $v = \sqrt{B/\rho}$ (liquid)
  - **Electromagnetic waves:** electric field $u = E(x,t)$, $v = c = 1/\sqrt{\mu_0 \epsilon_0}$ (light speed in vacuum)
  - **Quantum mechanics:** wave function $u = \psi(x,t)$, $v$ implicit in dispersion relation
  - **Gravity:** gravitational wave strain $u = h(x,t)$, $v = c$ (Einstein)
- Annotation: "Same equation; different substance oscillates; speed determined by medium properties"

**Purpose:** Emphasize the universality of wave phenomena; show how mastering sound prepares student for all wave physics to follow; set the stage for the connection between mechanics and electromagnetism.

**Callouts:** Referenced in "Integration and Synthesis" section at end of chapter.

## Supplementary Concepts (Not Illustrated, But Described)

1. **Bow Wakes:** V-shaped wake behind a fast boat (water waves), shock cone behind supersonic aircraft (sound waves), Cerenkov radiation cone around fast charged particle in medium (light). Could be shown in a triptych diagram but is conceptually secondary to understanding shock waves.

2. **Noise-Canceling Headphones:** Block diagram showing microphone on earcup → signal analyzer → phase inverter → speaker playing inverted sound → destructive interference in ear canal. Described verbally in Concept 4 but visual would strengthen intuition.

3. **End Correction in Tubes:** Open end of tube "bulges out" slightly; acoustic length = physical length + 0.6× radius. Shown as a small diagram overlaid on a clarinet or flute mouthpiece region. Mentioned but not illustrated in chapter due to space constraints.
