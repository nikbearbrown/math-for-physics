### Figure 1: Free-body diagram for friction on a horizontal surface

**Location:** Concept 1, Worked Example (a), (b), (c).

**Description:** 
- A square block on a horizontal surface.
- Applied force $P$ pointing rightward (horizontal).
- Weight $W = mg$ pointing downward.
- Normal force $N$ pointing upward.
- Friction force $f$ pointing leftward (opposite to $P$).
- Grid background with coordinate axes ($x$ rightward, $y$ upward).
- Three sub-diagrams showing friction force at different applied force magnitudes:
  - (a) $P = 20$ N: friction arrow shows $f_s = 20$ N.
  - (b) $P = 120$ N: friction arrow shows $f_s = 120$ N.
  - (c) $P = 180$ N: friction arrow shows $f_k = 118$ N (shorter arrow than $P$).

**Pedagogical purpose:** Illustrates the responsive nature of static friction and the transition to kinetic friction.

### Figure 2: Friction force vs. applied force graph

**Location:** Concept 1, as reference material (not in text but useful for visualization).

**Description:**
- Horizontal axis: Applied force $P$ (newtons), ranging from 0 to 200.
- Vertical axis: Friction force $f$ (newtons), ranging from 0 to 150.
- Static friction region (0 to 137 N): linear increase at 45-degree slope, labeled $f_s = P$ (for $P < \mu_s N$).
- Transition point at $P = 137$ N (vertical dashed line).
- Kinetic friction region (beyond 137 N): horizontal line at $f_k = 118$ N.
- Shaded regions: static (light blue), kinetic (light orange).
- Annotations: "$\mu_s N = 137$ N (max static)" and "$\mu_k N = 118$ N (kinetic)".

**Pedagogical purpose:** Shows visually why static friction "steps down" to kinetic friction, and why motion suddenly occurs.

### Figure 3: Skydiver falling through air — velocity vs. time

**Location:** Concept 2, showing approach to terminal velocity.

**Description:**
- Horizontal axis: Time $t$ (seconds), ranging from 0 to 100.
- Vertical axis: Velocity $v$ (m/s), ranging from 0 to 55.
- Curve: Exponential approach $v(t) = v_T(1 - e^{-t/\tau})$ with $v_T = 50$ m/s (spread-eagle position) and $\tau = 5$ s.
- Curve starts at origin, rises steeply initially, then flattens toward horizontal asymptote at $v_T = 50$ m/s.
- Dashed horizontal line at $v_T = 50$ m/s labeled "Terminal velocity".
- Vertical dashed line at $t = 5\tau = 25$ s, with annotation: "99% of terminal velocity".
- Shaded region under curve from $t = 0$ to $t = 30$ s.

**Pedagogical purpose:** Demonstrates that terminal velocity is a limit approached, not attained instantly.

### Figure 4: Free-body diagram for banked curve

**Location:** Concept 3, section "The banked curve — geometry does the work".

**Description:**
- Side view (cross-section) of a car on a banked curve.
- Car shown as a rectangle tilted at angle $\theta = 31°$ relative to horizontal.
- Banking surface tilted at $\theta$ from horizontal.
- Normal force $N$ drawn perpendicular to surface (pointing outward and upward).
- Components of $N$:
  - Vertical component $N \cos \theta$ pointing straight up, labeled.
  - Horizontal component $N \sin \theta$ pointing horizontally toward center of turn, labeled and drawn as an inward-pointing arrow.
- Weight $W = mg$ pointing straight down.
- Center of turn indicated by point to the left, with radius $r$ shown.
- Velocity vector $v$ pointing tangent to the circular path (into the page or out of the page, depending on view).
- Angle $\theta$ marked between surface and horizontal.

**Pedagogical purpose:** Shows how normal force components provide both vertical support and centripetal force.

### Figure 5: Ideal banking angle vs. speed

**Location:** Concept 3, as reference material.

**Description:**
- Horizontal axis: Speed $v$ (m/s), ranging from 10 to 50.
- Vertical axis: Banking angle $\theta$ (degrees), ranging from 0 to 45.
- Curve: $\theta = \tan^{-1}(v^2 / rg)$ for $r = 100$ m (Daytona example).
- Curve is concave upward, starting near zero angle at low speed, rising steeply at high speed.
- Specific points marked:
  - (20 m/s, 7.5°)
  - (24.3 m/s, 31°) — labeled "Daytona ideal"
  - (30 m/s, 45°)
- Annotation: "Banking angle increases with speed. Steeper banking for sharper curves or higher speeds."

**Pedagogical purpose:** Shows quantitatively how speed determines required banking for frictionless turn.

### Figure 6: Centripetal force components in circular motion

**Location:** Concept 3, section "Centripetal force — the requirement of circular motion" (as reference).

**Description:**
- Top-down (bird's-eye) view of circular path.
- Circle with radius $r$ shown.
- Car (or object) shown at one point on the circle.
- Velocity vector $v$ drawn tangent to the circle (perpendicular to radius).
- Centripetal acceleration $a_c$ drawn as an arrow pointing toward the center, labeled $a_c = v^2/r$.
- Centripetal force $F_c$ drawn as an arrow pointing toward the center, labeled $F_c = m v^2/r$.
- Radius $r$ drawn and labeled.

**Pedagogical purpose:** Clarifies that centripetal acceleration (and the force producing it) always points toward the center, perpendicular to velocity.

### Figure 7: Drag force vs. velocity

**Location:** Concept 2, section "What drag is (and why it matters)".

**Description:**
- Horizontal axis: Velocity $v$ (m/s), ranging from 0 to 40.
- Vertical axis: Drag force $F_D$ (newtons), ranging from 0 to 500.
- Curve: $F_D = \frac{1}{2} C \rho A v^2$ with $C = 0.5$, $\rho = 1.21$ kg/m³, $A = 0.1$ m² (small object).
- Parabolic curve, starting at origin, rising sharply (proportional to $v^2$).
- Tangent line at origin (linear approximation for very small $v$) shown as a light dashed line.
- Annotation: "Drag is proportional to $v^2$ for large objects at moderate speeds. For small $v$, linear approximation (Stokes' law) applies."

**Pedagogical purpose:** Illustrates the $v^2$ dependence and shows why the linear approximation fails at higher speeds.

## Figure creation notes

1. **Consistency:** Use the same color scheme across all figures (black for vectors, blue for accelerations, red for forces, green for surfaces/objects).

2. **Annotation:** All angles, forces, velocities, and accelerations should be labeled with symbols and (where helpful) numerical values from worked examples.

3. **Scale:** Ensure arrows are proportional to magnitude where size is meaningful (e.g., $P = 20$ N friction arrow should be much shorter than $P = 180$ N kinetic friction arrow).

4. **Grid backgrounds:** Use light gridlines in free-body diagrams to aid reading of component directions.

5. **Clarity over aesthetics:** Simplicity is preferred. A clean schematic is better than a photorealistic rendering if it obscures the physics.
