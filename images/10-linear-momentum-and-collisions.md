# Images and Figures: Linear Momentum and Collisions

## Figure locations and descriptions

### Opening figure: Falcon 9 ascending
[FIGURE: SpaceX Falcon 9 rocket ascending, clouds and blue sky in background, exhaust plume visible. Shows the Merlin engines at the base, each ejecting hot exhaust downward. Scale sense: rocket is ~70m tall, engines are ~1.5m diameter. Conveys the massive force generated by momentum conservation — thousands of kg/s of propellant ejected backward.]

*Caption:* The Falcon 9 ascends by ejecting 200+ kg of propellant per second at ~3 km/s. By conservation of momentum, the rocket gains an equal and opposite momentum forward. The rocket equation quantifies this exchange: the velocity gained depends on exhaust velocity and the ratio of initial to final mass.

### Figure 1.1: Momentum vs. kinetic energy comparison
[FIGURE: Two rows, three columns. Top row: bowling ball (10 kg, 1 m/s), car (1000 kg, 10 m/s), bullet (1 kg, 100 m/s). Middle row shows momentum values computed for each. Bottom row shows kinetic energy values. Color coding: momentum values highlighted in blue, kinetic energy in red. Illustrates that the same kinetic energy can have very different momentum, and vice versa.]

*Caption:* The bowling ball and bullet have the same momentum (10 kg·m/s) but different kinetic energies (5 J vs. 5000 J). This is why momentum, not kinetic energy, governs collisions and the forces involved in stopping moving objects.

### Figure 1.2: Force-time graph for impulse
[FIGURE: Two subplots. Left: Force (N) vs. Time (s). Top curve shows a large spike (high force, short duration). Bottom curve shows a gentler bump (lower force, longer duration). Both curves enclose the same area under the curve (shaded identically). Caption indicates both deliver the same impulse. Right subplot: Two scenarios labeled, one showing airbag deployment (low peak force, longer duration) vs. unprotected impact (high peak force, short duration).]

*Caption:* Impulse is the area under the force-time curve. An airbag extends the impact time (right), reducing peak force without changing the total impulse (and thus the momentum change). This is why airbags are effective in reducing injuries.

### Figure 1.3: Newton's cradle
[FIGURE: Five steel balls of equal size hanging from thin strings, arranged in a horizontal line touching each other. One ball on the left is lifted at an angle (frozen mid-swing, dashed position shown). Below the figure, a sequence showing the impact and the resulting motion on the opposite side. Annotations indicate momentum transfer and elastic collision.]

*Caption:* Newton's cradle demonstrates elastic collision and momentum conservation. When the left ball swings down and hits the stationary balls, momentum is transferred through the middle balls to the right ball, which swings out. The process reverses, and the left ball swings back.

### Figure 2.1: Collision scenarios
[FIGURE: Three horizontal rows, each showing a "before" and "after" state. Row 1 (perfectly inelastic): two blocks approach, then stick together as one block moving slower. Row 2 (elastic): two blocks approach, then bounce apart moving in opposite directions with reduced speeds. Row 3 (explosion): one stationary block, then splits into two blocks moving in opposite directions. Momentum vectors shown as arrows for each object, with labels indicating conservation. Color coding: before collision in blue, after in red.]

*Caption:* In perfectly inelastic collisions, objects stick and move slower (kinetic energy decreases). In elastic collisions, objects bounce and slow down but conserve kinetic energy. In explosions, momentum is conserved but kinetic energy increases (from internal potential energy).

### Figure 2.2: Center of mass concept
[FIGURE: Two panels. Left: a uniform rod with center of mass marked at its midpoint, with an upward force arrow. Right: a barbell (two unequal weights connected by a bar), with center of mass shifted toward the heavier weight, with an upward force arrow. Both are shown balanced on a pivot point at the center of mass location.]

*Caption:* The center of mass is the balance point of an object. A force applied at the center of mass causes pure translation (no rotation). The center of mass is always closer to the more massive parts of an object.

### Figure 2.3: Two-dimensional collision
[FIGURE: Top-down view of an intersection. A car (rectangular, moving northward, velocity vector pointing up) and a truck (larger rectangular, moving eastward, velocity vector pointing right) collide at the center. Before collision, their velocity vectors are shown. After collision, the combined wreckage is shown with a velocity vector pointing northeast at an angle. Momentum vectors (scaled) are shown before and after. Grid or compass rose indicates directions.]

*Caption:* When two vehicles collide at an angle, momentum is conserved in both x and y directions independently. The resulting velocity of the combined wreckage is the vector sum of the individual momenta, divided by total mass.

[FIGURE: Three snapshots of a rocket at different times. Early snapshot: rocket is bulky (full of fuel), with mass label $m_0$. *Caption:* As a rocket ejects propellant, its mass decreases and its velocity increases. The rocket equation relates the velocity change to the exhaust velocity and the mass ratio.

### Figure 3.2: Rocket equation and mass ratio
[FIGURE: Graph with mass ratio $m_0 / m_f$ on the horizontal axis (logarithmic or linear scale up to ~50) and $\Delta v$ on the vertical axis. A curve showing $\Delta v = v_e \ln(m_0 / m_f)$ is plotted for a specific exhaust velocity (e.g., $v_e = 2600$ m/s). Vertical dashed lines and annotations mark typical mass ratios for different mission types: low Earth orbit (mass ratio ~10-15), escape velocity (mass ratio ~50-100). Labels indicate Saturn V first stage, Space Shuttle, Falcon 9, and other rockets.]

*Caption:* The rocket equation shows that $\Delta v$ depends logarithmically on the mass ratio. To reach orbital velocity (mass ratio ~17) requires 17 times your final mass in propellant. Reaching escape velocity requires a much larger ratio.

### Figure 3.3: Center of mass in motion
[FIGURE: Sequence of snapshots showing two objects (different colors, different masses) at various positions. Momentum vectors are shown for each object at each time step. The center of mass is marked for each configuration, and shown to move at constant velocity across all snapshots (even though the objects move in different directions). Trajectory of center of mass is traced as a dashed line.]

*Caption:* The center of mass moves at constant velocity (when momentum is conserved), independent of the internal motions of the objects. In this sequence, the two objects move in different directions, but their center of mass traces a straight line at constant speed.

### Figure 3.4: Barbell center of mass calculation
[FIGURE: A barbell with two unequal weights. Left weight (larger, labeled 30 kg) at position x = 0. Right weight (smaller, labeled 20 kg) at position x = 2 m. The bar connecting them is shown. Below, a coordinate line with the weights marked and the center of mass location calculated and marked at x = 0.8 m. Shading or coloring indicates the weighted average.]

*Caption:* The center of mass of an unequal barbell is not at the geometric midpoint but closer to the heavier weight. Using $x_{CM} = (m_1 x_1 + m_2 x_2)/(m_1 + m_2) = (30 \times 0 + 20 \times 2)/(50) = 0.8$ m.

### Figure 3.5: Chicxulub crater
[FIGURE: Satellite or terrain image (or artist's rendering) of the Chicxulub crater in the Yucatan Peninsula. The crater is shown from above, roughly circular, with the rim visible. The scale is given (diameter ~180 km, depth ~900 m). An inset shows a cross-section of the crater depth. Annotation indicates the impact location and the direction of ejected material.]

### Supplementary: Worked example illustrations

**Figure S1.1: Ballistic pendulum**
[FIGURE: A bullet (small object) approaching a wooden block suspended by a string. Sequence: (1) bullet travels at velocity $v_{bullet}$, (2) bullet embeds in block, (3) block swings upward to height $h$. Momentum vectors and energy bars (kinetic and potential) shown for each stage.]

*Caption:* A ballistic pendulum uses momentum conservation (to find velocity just after impact) and energy conservation (to find the height swung). This demonstrates that different conservation laws apply in different contexts.

**Figure S2.1: Hockey puck elastic collision**
[FIGURE: Two pucks before collision (one moving, one at rest) and after collision (both moving, at different speeds). Velocity vectors shown. Momentum vectors for each puck and the total system shown as arrows, with labels confirming momentum conservation. Kinetic energy values labeled for before and after, showing conservation.]

*Caption:* In an elastic collision, both momentum and kinetic energy are conserved. The two pucks approach with total momentum $p_{total}$; after impact, they move at different speeds but the total momentum and total kinetic energy remain the same.

**Figure S2.2: Inelastic collision with energy loss**
[FIGURE: Two pucks colliding, sticking together. Momentum vectors shown before and after (conserved). Kinetic energy before collision labeled (sum of both pucks). Kinetic energy after labeled (lower, showing loss). The "lost" energy is annotated as heat, sound, and deformation.]

*Caption:* In a perfectly inelastic collision, momentum is conserved but kinetic energy is not. The lost kinetic energy is converted to heat, sound, and plastic deformation of the materials.

## Table of useful diagram elements

- **Momentum vectors:** Always shown as arrows, length proportional to magnitude, direction indicating direction of motion.
- **Velocity vectors:** Similar to momentum vectors but labeled $\vec{v}$, not $\vec{p}$.
- **Force vectors:** Often shown with different color or style (e.g., solid for external forces, dashed for internal forces).
- **Before/after notation:** Use "before" and "after" labels, or subscripts $i$ and $f$ (initial and final).
- **Color coding:** Consider using consistent colors for different object types (e.g., blue for object 1, red for object 2).
- **Grid/compass:** Include grid or compass rose for 2D collision problems to clarify directions.
