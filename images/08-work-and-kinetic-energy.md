# Chapter 8 Images — Work and Kinetic Energy

## Figure descriptions and pedagogical notes

### Figure 8.1 — Lawn mower with force at an angle

**Description:** A side-view diagram of a person pushing a lawn mower. The handle extends downward at 35 degrees below horizontal. A force arrow labeled $\vec{F}$ extends along the handle. A horizontal displacement arrow labeled $d$ shows the direction of motion.

**Pedagogical purpose:** Show that only the component of force parallel to displacement does work. A dashed vertical line from the tip of $\vec{F}$ to the horizontal displacement axis illustrates the component $F\cos\theta = F\cos(35°)$.

**What students should notice:** The force has a downward component, but the mower cannot move downward (the ground is in the way). That component does no work. Only the horizontal component matters.

**Suggested redrawing:** Add a work-vs-angle plot showing how $W = Fd\cos\theta$ changes as $\theta$ varies from 0 to 180 degrees. At $\theta = 0°$, $W$ is maximum. At $\theta = 90°$, $W = 0$. At $\theta = 180°$, $W$ is negative (force opposes motion).

---

### Figure 8.2 — Work along curved paths

**Description:** A curve connecting two points A and B. Multiple paths are drawn: a straight line, a circle bulging upward, a spiral, and others. An arrow at several points along each path shows a force vector $\vec{F}$.

**Pedagogical purpose:** Illustrate that for a constant force, the work integral depends only on endpoints (the displacement vector $\vec{r}_B - \vec{r}_A$), not on the path shape. For a variable force, the path matters.

**What students should notice:** Despite the different path lengths, if the force is constant, the work is the same. The calculation $W = \vec{F} \cdot (\vec{r}_B - \vec{r}_A)$ ignores the path entirely.

**Extension:** Show a spring force (magnitude increases with position) and illustrate that different paths give different work amounts. The path dependence becomes visible.

---

### Figure 8.3 — The pile driver

**Description:** A tall frame with a 500-kg hammer at height 5 m above a wooden pile. An arrow points downward showing $v = 0$ at the top. Below the hammer is a smaller diagram showing the hammer approaching the pile at the bottom, with an arrow labeled $v \approx 9.9$ m/s.

**Pedagogical purpose:** Connect gravitational work ($W = mgh = 24,500$ J) to the kinetic energy at impact ($K = \frac{1}{2}mv^2 = 24,500$ J).

**What students should notice:** The work done by gravity becomes the kinetic energy. No need to solve $v(t)$ or integrate acceleration. The energy equation gives the answer directly.

**Suggested addition:** Include a dashed vertical line with height markers at 2.5 m and 5 m, and plot $K(h)$ showing how kinetic energy increases linearly with height fallen.

---

### Figure 8.4 — Kinetic energy versus speed

**Description:** A graph with speed $v$ on the horizontal axis (0 to 15 m/s) and kinetic energy $K$ on the vertical axis (0 to 1,000 J, for a 10-kg object). The parabola $K = 5v^2$ is drawn.

**Pedagogical purpose:** Show that kinetic energy grows as the *square* of speed. Doubling the speed quadruples the kinetic energy.

**What students should notice:** The curve is not linear. Small changes in speed at low velocities do little to kinetic energy; at high velocities, small changes matter a lot. This is why car safety is speed-dependent.

**Suggested addition:** Mark points at $v = 5$ m/s ($K = 125$ J) and $v = 10$ m/s ($K = 500$ J), showing that doubling from 5 to 10 m/s increases $K$ by a factor of 4.

---

### Figure 8.5 — Constant force along curved surface

**Description:** A marble sliding down a smooth, curved track (not a circle, but a smooth wiggle) from height $h_1$ to height $h_2$. Gravity is shown as a downward vector. The normal force (perpendicular to the surface at each point) is shown at three locations along the curve.

**Pedagogical purpose:** Illustrate that the normal force, perpendicular to motion, does zero work. Only the component of gravity parallel to motion (in the direction of descending height) contributes.

**What students should notice:** The shape of the track is irrelevant to the final speed. Only the height difference matters. The work-energy theorem gives $v_2$ without needing to know the path.

**Suggested redrawing:** Show the same curved path but with friction added. Mark the work done by friction (negative) and show that the final speed is lower. This previews the non-conservative nature of friction.

---

### Figure 8.6 — Power plant and solar panels

**Description:** Left side: a large cooling tower labeled "500 MW coal plant." Right side: a rooftop with solar panels labeled "5 kW on sunny days, 1 kW on cloudy days." Include a timeline at the bottom showing energy delivered over 1 second, 1 hour, and 1 day.

**Pedagogical purpose:** Contrast the rate of energy flow (power) with the total energy. Same total energy can be delivered at vastly different rates.

**What students should notice:** Power is not energy. The 500-MW plant delivers 500 million joules every second. The 5-kW solar panel delivers 5,000 joules per second — 100,000 times less power, but the same total energy from both over appropriate times.

---

### Figure 8.7 — Pull-up power calculation

**Description:** Side-view stick figure doing a pull-up, arms at three positions: bottom (full extension), middle (arms bent), and top (chin above bar). The height gained is marked as 0.6 m, and the time interval is labeled 0.8 s.

**Pedagogical purpose:** Calculate power from $P = \frac{W}{t}$ where $W = mgh$.

**What students should notice:** An 80-kg person doing a 0.6 m pull-up in 0.8 s produces about 588 watts — less than a typical car engine (100+ kW) but more than a 100-W light bulb. The number is relatable.

---

### Figure 8.8 — Force and velocity vectors for power

**Description:** Three scenarios showing a force vector $\vec{F}$ and a velocity vector $\vec{v}$ at different angles. Left: $\vec{F}$ and $\vec{v}$ parallel ($\theta = 0°$), labeled "Positive power." Middle: perpendicular ($\theta = 90°$), labeled "Zero power." Right: antiparallel ($\theta = 180°$), labeled "Negative power."

**Pedagogical purpose:** Show that $P = \vec{F} \cdot \vec{v} = Fv\cos\theta$ depends on the angle between force and velocity.

**What students should notice:** When a force acts in the direction of motion, power is positive and energy flows in. When perpendicular, no work happens (e.g., normal force on level ground). When opposite, power is negative and energy is removed (e.g., friction).

---

### Figure 8.9 — Spring force versus displacement

**Description:** A horizontal axis showing displacement $x$ (with 0 at equilibrium, positive to the right, negative to the left). The spring force $F = -kx$ is plotted: a straight line passing through the origin with negative slope.

**Pedagogical purpose:** Show that spring force is linear in displacement. Shading under the line represents work.

**What students should notice:** The "area" under the $F$-versus-$x$ graph from $x = 0$ to $x = x_B$ is a triangle with "area" $\frac{1}{2}kx_B^2$, which equals the work magnitude $|W|$. The sign (negative work by the spring during stretching) is captured by the force vector pointing opposite to the displacement direction.

---

### Figure 8.10 — Energy flow in an archer's bow

**Description:** Three snapshots. Left: bow at rest, labeled "No stored energy." Middle: bow drawn, with elastic limbs curved and a label "Elastic potential energy $U$." Right: arrow in flight, with a label "Kinetic energy $K = U$ (at release)."

**Pedagogical purpose:** Connect the stored elastic energy in the bow to the kinetic energy of the arrow after release.

**What students should notice:** Energy is conserved; it changes form from elastic potential to kinetic. The arrow's speed depends on the bow's stiffness and the draw distance.

---

### Figure 8.11 — Car climbing a hill at constant speed

**Description:** A car on an inclined plane (angle $\theta$ with horizontal). The engine is shown as a labeled power source. Gravity acts downward. Air resistance acts horizontally backward. At constant speed, acceleration is zero, so forces balance.

**Pedagogical purpose:** Show that the power from the engine balances the power dissipated by gravity and drag: $P_{\text{engine}} = P_{\text{gravity}} + P_{\text{drag}} = mgv\sin\theta + F_{\text{drag}} \cdot v$.

**What students should notice:** On a steep hill, most engine power goes to fighting gravity. On a level road with low drag, the power needed is small. The relationship $P = \vec{F} \cdot \vec{v}$ is the key.

---

## Suggestions for student visualization activities

### Activity 1: Measure work on your body

Students estimate the work they do climbing stairs. Measure height gained, count steps, estimate time. Calculate $W = mgh$ and $P = W/t$. Compare to a typical light-bulb power (60–100 W). The surprise: stair-climbing is brief but intense.

### Activity 2: Springs and energy

Using a spring scale and a ruler, students measure the force needed to stretch a spring at different displacements. Plot $F$ versus $x$, confirm $F \propto x$. Calculate the spring constant $k$ from the slope. Then integrate to find the work done: $W = \frac{1}{2}kx_{\max}^2$.

### Activity 3: Power ratings on household devices

Collect the power ratings (in watts) from appliances: microwave (1000 W), hair dryer (1500 W), phone charger (5–20 W), light bulb (10–100 W). Run them for various times and estimate the daily or monthly energy consumption in kilowatt-hours. The bill confirms the calculation.

### Activity 4: Video analysis of a bounce

Record a ball bouncing (or find one online). Use frame-by-frame analysis to estimate the speed just before and just after impact. Calculate the change in kinetic energy. This equals the magnitude of work the ground did (negative during compression, positive during extension). If the bounce is less than perfectly elastic, where did the energy go?
