## Part 2: Translational Equilibrium

**[FIGURE 13-02: Free-Body Diagram of a Pan with Two Strings]**
Diagram showing a small pan (represented as a circle) suspended by two strings at angles. Labels:
- String 1 (5 cm long): tension $T_1$ at angle $\alpha_1$ above horizontal
- String 2 (10 cm long): tension $T_2$ at angle $\alpha_2$ above horizontal
- Weight $w$ pointing straight down from the pan's center
The diagram shows a right triangle formed by the ceiling separation and string lengths, with angles marked. Underneath, a component vector diagram decomposes $T_1$ and $T_2$ into $x$ and $y$ components. Arrow annotations show the direction of each component and which forces they balance.

**[FIGURE 13-03: Comparison—First Condition Only vs. Both Conditions]**
Two panels side by side:
- Left: A door being pushed with equal forces at equal distances on opposite sides. Arrows labeled $F$ point into the door from both sides. Below: "$\sum F = 0$ ✓ But the door rotates!" 
- Right: The same door with forces adjusted so that $\sum \tau = 0$ as well. The two forces are now on the same side of the hinge. Below: "Both conditions satisfied. The door stays still."
The figure teaches: zero net force is not enough.

## Part 3: Rotational Equilibrium

**[FIGURE 13-04: Torque Depends on the Pivot]**
A uniform rod with a force $\vec{F}$ applied at a point. Three different pivot points are shown (marked as P₁, P₂, P₃). For each, the lever arm $r$ is drawn as a perpendicular from the pivot to the line of action of the force. The three lever arms have different lengths. Annotation:
- "Torque about P₁: $\tau_1 = r_1 F \sin \theta_1$"
- "Torque about P₂: $\tau_2 = r_2 F \sin \theta_2$"
- Caption: "The same force produces different torques about different pivots. But if all external forces sum to zero, then the total torque is the same about any pivot."

**[FIGURE 13-05: The Meter Stick on a Fulcrum]**
Diagram of a uniform meter stick (1 m long, marked with centimeter ticks) balanced on a fulcrum at the 30 cm mark. Hanging masses:
- 50 g at the 10 cm mark (20 cm to the left of the fulcrum): arrow down labeled $w_1 = 0.50$ N
- 75 g at the 70 cm mark (40 cm to the right): arrow down labeled $w_2 = 0.75$ N
- Weight of the stick itself: arrow at the 50 cm mark (20 cm to the right) labeled $w = 1.50$ N
- Unknown mass at the 100 cm mark (70 cm to the right): arrow down labeled $w_3 = ?$
- Normal force at the fulcrum: arrow up labeled $N$
At the bottom, a free-body diagram shows the lever arms relative to the fulcrum with distance markings. Calculation shown: $m_3 \approx 317$ g.

## Part 4: Problem-Solving

**[FIGURE 13-06: Free-Body Diagram of a Ladder Against a Wall]**
A uniform ladder of length $L = 5.0$ m leaning against a frictionless wall at angle $\beta$ above the floor. The ladder is drawn in light gray; the forces are in bold:
- At the top (against the wall): horizontal arrow pointing away from the wall, labeled $F_w$ (normal force from wall)
- At the bottom (on the floor): vertical arrow pointing up labeled $N$ (normal force from floor), and horizontal arrow pointing toward the wall labeled $f$ (friction)
- At the center: vertical arrow pointing down labeled $w = 400$ N (weight)
A coordinate system is shown with $x$ horizontal and $y$ vertical. The contact points are marked with circles. A separate inset diagram shows the geometry: the angle $\beta$, the height of contact with the wall ($h = L \cos \beta$), and the horizontal distance from the wall to the floor contact ($d = L \sin \beta$).

**[FIGURE 13-07: Pivot Point Selection Strategy]**
Two panels showing the same system (a beam supported by two pillars with a load in the middle):
- Left: Pivot at the left support. All three forces create torques; the equations are complex.
- Right: Pivot at the left support, but with annotations showing: "The force $N_{\text{left}}$ acts at the pivot—its torque is zero. Choose your pivot wisely."
Caption: "The choice of pivot is a strategic decision. A good choice simplifies the algebra."

## Part 5: Stress and Strain

**[FIGURE 13-08: Tensile Stress and Strain]**
A diagram showing:
- Left panel: A rod of length $L_0$ and cross-sectional area $A$. Arrows labeled $F$ point outward from both ends (tensile force).
- Right panel: The same rod under load, now stretched to length $L = L_0 + \Delta L$. The elongation $\Delta L$ is marked with a double-arrow, and the new length is indicated.
Below: Definitions in boxes:
- Stress = $F / A$ (units: Pa)
- Strain = $\Delta L / L_0$ (dimensionless)
- Young's modulus: $Y = \text{Stress} / \text{Strain}$

**[FIGURE 13-09: Three Types of Deformation]**
Three columns, each showing a cube being deformed:
- Left: **Tensile stress**. The cube is stretched along one axis by forces pulling on opposite faces. The dimension parallel to the force increases; the other dimensions decrease (Poisson effect).
- Center: **Bulk stress**. The cube is squeezed from all sides by pressure (arrows pointing inward on all six faces). The volume decreases uniformly.
- Right: **Shear stress**. The cube is sheared by forces acting parallel to opposite faces. The cube becomes a parallelogram. One pair of opposite faces remains parallel; the other pair is at an angle.
Annotations show which elastic modulus applies to each case.

**[FIGURE 13-10: The Steel Cable Under Load]**
A vertical steel cable hanging from a ceiling. The cable is drawn at an exaggerated scale to make the load visible. Labels:
- Ceiling at the top
- Cable length $L_0 = 2.0$ m (original)
- Load (platform) hanging from the bottom, with arrows labeled $Mg = 5.4$ kN pointing down
- Elongation $\Delta L = 1.8$ mm shown as a small double-arrow (drawn larger than reality for visibility)
- Cross-sectional area $A = 3.0 \times 10^{-5}$ m² (about the size of a pencil tip)
- Calculation box: Stress = $1.8 \times 10^8$ Pa; Strain = $9 \times 10^{-4}$; $\Delta L = 1.8$ mm

## Part 6: Material Limits

**[FIGURE 13-11: Stress-Strain Diagram for a Ductile Metal]**
A graph with stress (vertical axis, in MPa) and strain (horizontal axis, dimensionless) plotted. The curve shows:
1. **Linear elastic region** (origin to point H): A straight line with slope $Y$ (Young's modulus). Point H is labeled "Linearity limit" or "Proportionality limit."
2. **Nonlinear elastic region** (H to E): The curve bends upward. Point E is labeled "Elastic limit" or "Yield point."
3. **Plastic region** (E to F): The curve continues to rise but may plateau or even dip slightly as the material thins. Point F is labeled "Breaking point" or "Ultimate stress."
4. **Fracture** (beyond F): The curve ends.

Annotations show:
- A hypothetical unloading path from point P in the plastic region: a dashed line parallel to the initial slope, leading to a permanent offset (permanent strain). This illustrates that once you leave the elastic region, the material does not return to zero strain when the load is removed.
- "Before P: elastic (recoverable). After E: plastic (permanent)."
- Numerical values for steel: Elastic limit ~250 MPa, ultimate stress ~400 MPa.

**[FIGURE 13-12: Brittle vs. Ductile Materials]**
Two stress-strain curves plotted on the same axes for comparison:
- **Steel** (ductile, blue curve): Long linear region, enters plastic flow gradually, shows significant strain before breaking (strain ~0.1 before fracture).
- **Glass** (brittle, red curve): Linear region extends almost to the breaking point. Fracture is abrupt with little warning and minimal plastic deformation. Strain at break ~0.01.
Caption: "Ductile materials warn you (they deform noticeably before breaking). Brittle materials break suddenly."

## Part 7: Integration

**[FIGURE 13-13: Inside a Bridge—Force Distribution]**
A cross-sectional diagram of a suspension bridge box-beam showing:
- Main cable from above, showing how tension is distributed to the suspender cables
- Suspender cables (vertical) connecting the main cable to the top of the box-beam
- The box-beam itself, with internal stress contours showing:
  - Compression at the top (color-coded red, with "C" indicating compression)
  - Tension at the bottom (color-coded blue, with "T" indicating tension)
- Roadway on top of the beam with distributed load (vehicles, pedestrians, own weight)
- Support at the end (next span)
Annotations:
- "The cable is in tension: $\sum F = 0$ and $\sum \tau = 0$ about every point."
- "The beam bends: compression above, tension below. The Young's modulus of the steel determines how much it deflects."
- "Engineering is the art of satisfying both conditions simultaneously."

## Part 8: Scale Shift

**[FIGURE 13-14: From Atomic Scale to Structural Scale]**
A four-panel sequence showing scale escalation:
1. **Atomic level** (~1 nm): Two atoms connected by a spring representing the atomic bond. Label: "Bond stiffness: $k$"
2. **Microscopic level** (~1 μm): A thin crystal shown as a lattice of atoms. Label: "Young's modulus: $Y = ka_0 / A$ (thousands of bonds in parallel)"
3. **Macroscopic level** (~1 m): A steel cable. Label: "Deformation: $\Delta L = (F/A) \cdot (L_0 / Y)$ (same modulus, larger scale)"
4. **Structural level** (~100 m): A suspension bridge with a 1 km main span. Label: "Real-world constraints: the cable stretches 10 meters under load—engineers must accommodate this deflection in the design."

## Additional Annotations

**[FIGURE 13-15: Common Error—Confusing Stress and Strain]**
Two panels showing the same material (e.g., a rubber band) under identical stress:
- Thin rubber band: stretches a lot (large strain)
- Thick rubber band: stretches less (smaller strain)
Caption: "Same material, same stress, different deformations! Strain depends on both the material AND the geometry (cross-sectional area and original length). Young's modulus alone does not predict the absolute deformation—you need to know the dimensions."

**[FIGURE 13-16: The Elastic Limit as a Practical Boundary]**
A diagram showing a material property curve (e.g., Young's modulus) versus stress. A horizontal line marks the elastic limit. Annotations:
- Below the line: "Design here (safety margin). The material will bounce back."
- Above the line: "Never design here. Permanent damage and failure."
- The practical design limit is typically 50% of the ultimate stress, to leave room for uncertainties, dynamic loading, and material degradation over time.

## Production Notes

- All diagrams should use consistent color coding: red for compression, blue for tension, gray for neutral.
- Arrows indicating forces should be proportional to their magnitude where possible (a 10 N force gets a longer arrow than a 5 N force).
- Free-body diagrams should use solid lines for the object and solid arrows for forces, with clear labels for magnitude and direction.
- Stress-strain curves should use a smooth line (not jagged) to indicate continuous behavior, even in the plastic region.
- Scale shift figures should use insets or separated panels to avoid visual confusion.
