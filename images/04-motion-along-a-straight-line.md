### Concept 1: Position, Velocity, and Acceleration

**[FIGURE: Horizontal number line with cyclist at position +5 m and origin at x = 0]**
- **Caption:** A cyclist's position is measured relative to a chosen origin. Here, the cyclist is 5 meters to the right of the origin (positive direction).
- **Placement:** After first definition of position
- **Pedagogical purpose:** Visualizes the concept of position as a location on a coordinate axis.

**[FIGURE: Position-versus-time graph showing position increasing nonlinearly]**
- **Caption:** Position as a function of time for an object undergoing constant acceleration. The curve is a parabola, characteristic of $x(t) = x_0 + v_0 t + \frac{1}{2}at^2$.
- **Placement:** After discussion of position functions
- **Pedagogical purpose:** Shows how to read and interpret position-time graphs; visually suggests that instantaneous velocity is the slope at any point.

**[FIGURE: Position-time graph with secant lines becoming tangent line]**
- **Caption:** As the time interval Δ*t* shrinks (from Δ*t₁* to Δ*t₂* to Δ*t₃*), the secant line slope approaches the tangent line slope. The tangent line's slope is the instantaneous velocity.
- **Placement:** After definition of instantaneous velocity
- **Pedagogical purpose:** Visualizes the limit process by which average velocity becomes instantaneous velocity.

**[FIGURE: Three position-time curves with different shapes]**
- **Caption:** Three objects with different motion profiles. (Left) Constant velocity (straight line). (Center) Constant positive acceleration (upward-curving parabola). (Right) Constant negative acceleration (downward-curving parabola).
- **Placement:** After discussion of acceleration's effect on position
- **Pedagogical purpose:** Shows how the curvature of the *x*-*t* graph reflects the sign of acceleration.

**[FIGURE: Velocity-versus-time graph, linear with positive slope]**
- **Caption:** Velocity as a function of time for an object with constant acceleration. The graph is a straight line with slope equal to the acceleration.
- **Placement:** After discussion of instantaneous acceleration
- **Pedagogical purpose:** Shows that instantaneous acceleration is the slope of the velocity-time graph.

### Concept 2: Kinematic Equations

**[FIGURE: Velocity-time graph showing the area under the curve equals displacement]**
- **Caption:** The area under the velocity-time graph equals displacement. For constant acceleration (straight line), the area is a trapezoid with height Δ*t* and parallel sides *v₀* and *v*.
- **Placement:** After deriving the kinematic equations
- **Pedagogical purpose:** Shows the geometric interpretation of $\Delta x = \bar{v} \, t$ and justifies why displacement depends on average velocity.

**[FIGURE: Car on highway with distance markers]**
- **Caption:** A car decelerating from 30 m/s over a distance of 64.3 meters to a stop. The question: will it stop before the traffic light?
- **Placement:** Before the stopping car worked example
- **Pedagogical purpose:** Contextualizes the kinematic problem with a realistic scenario.

**[FIGURE: Velocity versus distance graph for the braking car, showing v² = v₀² + 2a(Δx)]**
- **Caption:** Final velocity depends on both the initial velocity and the distance over which acceleration acts. Notice that velocity decreases as a function of distance (not time).
- **Placement:** After the stopping car example
- **Pedagogical purpose:** Shows why the $v^2 = v_0^2 + 2a\Delta x$ equation is the right choice when time is unknown.

### Concept 3: Free Fall

**[FIGURE: Two objects (a bowling ball and a feather) falling in a vacuum at the same acceleration]**
- **Caption:** In a vacuum, a bowling ball and a feather fall side by side with the same acceleration *g*. The feather is not "slowing down" due to gravity; both experience the same gravitational acceleration. In air, air resistance complicates the picture.
- **Placement:** After first discussion of free fall
- **Pedagogical purpose:** Challenges the intuition that heavier objects fall faster and establishes that free fall is universal (same *g* for all masses).

**[FIGURE: Ball thrown upward, showing positions at equal time intervals]**
- **Caption:** A ball thrown upward with initial velocity 20 m/s, shown at 0.5-second intervals. The spacing between positions decreases as the ball rises (velocity decreases) and increases as it falls (velocity increases again).
- **Placement:** Before the upward throw worked example
- **Pedagogical purpose:** Visualizes the parabolic trajectory and the symmetry of upward and downward motion.

**[FIGURE: Height versus time for a ball thrown upward, parabola with maximum]**
- **Caption:** Height as a function of time for a ball thrown upward with initial velocity 20 m/s. The maximum height occurs at the instant when velocity becomes zero.
- **Placement:** After the upward throw example
- **Pedagogical purpose:** Shows how to read the maximum from the graph and connects to the worked calculation.

**[FIGURE: Baumgartner's altitude versus time for first 30 seconds, curved downward]**
- **Caption:** Felix Baumgartner's altitude during the first 30 seconds of free fall (neglecting air resistance). The curve is concave downward because the rate of descent accelerates continuously.
- **Placement:** After Baumgartner revisited example
- **Pedagogical purpose:** Applies the free-fall mathematics to the opening scenario; shows that the idealized model fits the real situation reasonably well for the first 30 seconds.

**[FIGURE: Altitude versus time for Baumgartner with air resistance (actual) and without (free fall model)]**
- **Caption:** Comparison of Baumgartner's actual altitude (blue curve) with the free-fall model prediction (red curve). The free-fall model overestimates acceleration because it ignores air resistance, which increases with speed.
- **Placement:** After discussion of air resistance effects
- **Pedagogical purpose:** Shows the limitations of the constant-acceleration model and motivates the need for more sophisticated models.

## Integration of Figures with Text

### Optimal Placement Strategy

1. **Opening figure (Baumgartner):** Immediately after hook to establish stakes.
2. **Definition figures:** Position-time and velocity-time graphs accompanying the definitions of velocity and acceleration.
3. **Derivation figures:** Graphs showing limits, areas under curves, geometric interpretations of equations.
4. **Worked-example figures:** Sketches and graphs specific to each example (stopping car, ball, skydiver).
5. **Comparison figure:** Free fall versus air resistance, showing model breakdown.

### Caption Style

- Brief title identifying the figure
- Descriptive caption (1–2 sentences) explaining what is shown
- Pedagogical purpose (1 sentence) explaining why this figure illuminates the concept

## Notes on Visualization Philosophy

The figures in this chapter are meant to serve the **Attenborough × Feynman** voice: they show *mechanism* first. Rather than abstract diagrams, we prefer:
- Real-world scenarios (Baumgartner, car, baseball)
- Graphs that make the mathematics visible (area under curve = displacement)
- Sequences showing how things change (ball at intervals, Baumgartner over time)

Figures are not decoration. Each figure should answer a question the text has raised or provide visual confirmation of an algebraic derivation.
