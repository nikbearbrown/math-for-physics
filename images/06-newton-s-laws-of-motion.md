### Concept 1 — Newton's First Law

**[FIGURE: Three panels in sequence.

Panel A: A puck on an ice hockey table with the air system off. An arrow shows a force pushing the puck. The puck is in motion, shown with a velocity arrow.

Panel B: Same puck, same friction force magnitude, but the air system is on (creating a nearly frictionless surface). The same pushing force is applied. The puck is in motion with a larger velocity arrow.

Panel C: A puck gliding on the air hockey table with no force applied. It continues moving in a straight line at constant velocity, shown by a velocity arrow of constant length.

Caption: The air hockey table demonstrates Newton's first law. When the air is off (A), friction (a force) slows the puck quickly. When the air is on (B and C), friction nearly disappears. The puck then moves at constant velocity with no applied force. This constant motion (not "natural" rest) is what the first law describes.]**

**Alternative source:** Photographs of air hockey tables, or a diagram if photos are unavailable. The principle is clear: friction → changing motion; no friction → constant motion.

### Concept 2 — Newton's Second Law

**[FIGURE: A shopping cart with variable push force.

Three scenarios side by side:

Scenario A: Cart with mass $m$. An arrow labeled $F$ points forward (to the right). The cart has acceleration $a$.

Scenario B: Same cart, but the pushing force is labeled $2F$. The cart's acceleration is labeled $2a$. (Emphasizing the proportionality of acceleration to force.)

Scenario C: Cart with the same force $F$ as in Scenario A, but loaded with twice the mass. The acceleration is labeled $a/2$. (Emphasizing the inverse proportionality of acceleration to mass.)

Caption: Newton's second law in everyday experience. The same force applied to the same empty cart produces acceleration $a$. Double the force and the acceleration doubles. Double the load (mass) and the acceleration halves. The quantitative relationship is $\vec{F} = m\vec{a}$.]**

### Concept 3 — Newton's Third Law

**[FIGURE: A swimmer pushing off a pool wall.

Two panels:

Panel A: The swimmer is shown facing the wall, hands extended forward, pushing on the wall with force $\vec{F}_{\text{swimmer on wall}}$ (arrow pointing into the wall, to the left).

Panel B: By Newton's third law, the wall exerts an equal and opposite force $\vec{F}_{\text{wall on swimmer}}$ (arrow pointing away from the wall, to the right). The swimmer accelerates to the right.

Caption: The swimmer pushes the wall with force $F$. By Newton's third law, the wall pushes the swimmer with force $F$ in the opposite direction. The wall does not accelerate (it is attached to the Earth) because the swimmer's push acts on the wall, not on the wall itself... Wait, rephrase: The wall's push acts on the swimmer, causing the swimmer to accelerate. The swimmer's push acts on the wall, but the wall's enormous mass (effectively infinite, as it is fixed to the Earth) prevents it from accelerating noticeably.]**

**[FIGURE: A rocket expelling gas.

The rocket is shown as a simple shape (nose up, bell-shaped engine at the bottom). A plume of hot gas is shown exiting the bell, with an arrow labeled $\vec{F}_{\text{rocket on gas}}$ pointing downward (the rocket pushes gas downward).

By Newton's third law, the gas exerts a reaction force on the rocket, labeled $\vec{F}_{\text{gas on rocket}}$, pointing upward.

A caption notes: "This reaction force, called *thrust*, accelerates the rocket upward. The rocket does not push on the ground or the air behind it; it accelerates by expelling mass from its engine."

Caption: A rocket creates thrust by expelling hot gas downward at high velocity. The rocket pushes on the gas; the gas pushes on the rocket with equal and opposite force. This is action-reaction. The rocket works even better in a vacuum because there is no air resistance to oppose the expelled gas.]**

**[FIGURE: Professor with cart.

A professor stands to the left of a cart. The professor's feet push backward on the floor with force $\vec{F}_{\text{foot}}$ (arrow pointing left). By Newton's third law, the floor pushes forward on the professor with force $\vec{F}_{\text{floor}}$ (arrow pointing right).

The professor, cart, and equipment move to the right together, accelerating at $a$.

Annotation shows the free-body diagram for the system: forward floor force (150 N), friction force (24 N to the left), net force (126 N to the right).

Caption: The professor's action is a push backward on the floor. The floor's reaction is a push forward on the professor. The professor accelerates forward because of the floor's push. This is Newton's third law: action and reaction, both present, both equal and opposite, both real forces — but acting on different objects (professor vs. floor).]**

## Diagram conventions and specifications

1. **Free-body diagrams:** Always show forces as arrows emanating from a point (representing the object). Label each force with magnitude and direction. Do not include the object's velocity or trajectory in a free-body diagram — only forces.

2. **Vector notation:** Use $\hat{i}$ and $\hat{j}$ for unit vectors when appropriate. Show both component form and magnitude+direction form.

3. **Coordinate systems:** Always establish a coordinate system (x-axis, y-axis, with arrows showing positive direction). In incline problems, tilted axes (parallel and perpendicular to the surface) are often clearer.

4. **Colors:** Use consistent colors:
   - Applied/contact forces: blue
   - Gravity/weight: red
   - Normal forces: green
   - Friction: orange
   - Tension: purple
   - Net force: black (or bold)

5. **Scale:** Arrows should be scaled proportionally to force magnitude when vectors are added graphically. (E.g., if $F_1 = 10$ N and $F_2 = 20$ N, the arrow for $F_2$ should be twice as long as the arrow for $F_1$.)

>

## Notes for the illustrator

- Keep diagrams clean and uncluttered.
- Use thin lines for construction; bold lines for forces.
- Label all quantities with SI units.
- For 3D scenarios (like the rocket sled), use isometric or perspective view to clarify spatial relationships.
- Ensure all free-body diagrams are drawn to scale (force arrows proportional to magnitude).
