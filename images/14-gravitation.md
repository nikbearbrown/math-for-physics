# Images and Figures: Chapter 14 — Gravitation

## Figure references and descriptions

The chapter references several diagrams that should be provided by illustration. These are standard figures, widely published in physics textbooks. Where a figure is named here, it should be sourced and placed in the chapter at the indicated section.

### Concept 1 — Newton's Law of Universal Gravitation

**[FIGURE: Cavendish torsion balance setup]**
- Shows the apparatus: wooden rod suspended by a quartz fiber, small lead spheres at the ends of the rod, larger lead spheres positioned above and below.
- Includes an inset showing the twist of the fiber as the large masses attract the small ones.
- Caption: "Cavendish's torsion balance measured the gravitational force between lead masses. The minute twist of the quartz fiber allowed him to calculate G to one part in 1000 — a feat not significantly improved for more than a century."

**[FIGURE: Gravitational force vector diagram]**
- Two masses with force vectors pointing from each toward the other (Newton's third law pair).
- Shows the distance r marked between their centers.
- Caption: "The gravitational force on mass 1 due to mass 2 points from 1 toward 2 and has magnitude $F = Gm_1m_2/r^2$. By Newton's third law, the force on 2 due to 1 is equal and opposite."

**[FIGURE: Inverse square law with sphere]**
- A point mass at the center with a sphere around it at radius r, divided into rings to show that area increases with $r^2$.
- Inset shows a smaller sphere (radius 2r) with one-quarter the surface area density.
- Caption: "Any influence spreading radially from a point follows an inverse square law because the surface area of a sphere is proportional to $r^2$. If the total influence is constant, then influence per unit area is proportional to $1/r^2$."

### Concept 2 — Gravitational Potential Energy and Escape Velocity

**[FIGURE: Gravitational potential energy curve]**
- Graph with r on the x-axis (from 0 to, say, 6 Earth radii), U on the y-axis (negative values).
- Curve shows $U(r) = -GMm/r$, starting at very negative values close to r=0 and approaching 0 asymptotically as r increases.
- Horizontal line at U=0 marked to show the reference point at infinity.
- Caption: "Gravitational potential energy $U = -GMm/r$ is always negative (for positive masses). The more distant the masses, the less negative the energy, until it approaches zero at infinity."

**[FIGURE: Energy diagram for escape]**
- Two curves on the same graph: total energy E (horizontal line) and potential energy U (the $-1/r$ curve).
- The difference between E and U at any radius is the kinetic energy K.
- Shaded region shows the allowed region (where K ≥ 0).
- For E=0 (escape), the allowed region extends to infinity.
- For E<0 (bound orbit), the allowed region is limited (between two turning points).
- Caption: "For an object to escape to infinity, its total energy must be zero or positive. If total energy is negative, the object is bound in the region where K ≥ 0."

### Concept 3 — Orbits: Kepler's Laws and Orbital Mechanics

**[FIGURE: Circular orbit geometry]**
- Overhead view of Earth with a satellite in circular orbit at radius r.
- Velocity vector shown tangent to the orbit.
- Acceleration vector and gravitational force vector shown pointing toward Earth's center.
- Caption: "In a circular orbit, the gravitational force provides exactly the centripetal force needed to curve the satellite's path. The satellite is in free fall toward Earth, but its sideways velocity keeps it in orbit."

**[FIGURE: Elliptical orbit with Kepler's Second Law]**
- An ellipse with the Sun at one focus (not centered).
- Three regions swept out by a radius from the Sun, labeled A1, A2, A3, all equal in area.
- The planet is shown moving faster near perihelion (close) and slower near aphelion (far).
- Caption: "Kepler's Second Law: A planet sweeps out equal areas in equal times. The planet moves faster when close to the Sun and slower when far away, conserving angular momentum."

**[FIGURE: The four conic sections]**
- Four separate diagrams showing: (1) circle (e=0), (2) ellipse (0<e<1), (3) parabola (e=1), (4) hyperbola (e>1).
- Each labeled with the eccentricity and the type of orbit (bound or unbounded).
- All shown relative to a focus point (representing the attracting mass).
- Caption: "An object's trajectory in a gravitational field is one of four conic sections, determined by the total mechanical energy. Negative energy gives elliptical orbits; zero gives parabolic; positive gives hyperbolic."

**[FIGURE: Halley's Comet orbit]**
- Ellipse showing the comet's path around the Sun.
- Sun at one focus.
- Perihelion marked at 0.586 AU.
- Aphelion marked at 35 AU.
- Earth's orbit shown for scale (1 AU radius).
- Caption: "Halley's Comet travels on a highly elliptical orbit with a 75-year period. Its perihelion is inside Venus's orbit; its aphelion extends beyond Neptune."

### Synthesis section

**[FIGURE: Spacetime curvature (conceptual)]**
- Two-dimensional representation of space as a curved surface.
- A massive object (shown as a sphere) sitting on the surface, creating a dimple or well.
- A smaller object moving along the curved surface, following a path around the well.
- Label noting this is a 2D analogy for 3D curved spacetime.
- Caption: "In general relativity, mass curves spacetime. Objects follow geodesics (shortest paths) through curved spacetime, and we perceive these paths as gravitational orbits. This is not a literal picture of spacetime but a useful analogy."

## Visual conventions

- Vectors are shown as arrows, with magnitude indicated by arrow length.
- Gravitational force vectors always point from one mass toward the other.
- Orbital diagrams show the Sun or Earth at a focus (for bound orbits) or at one point of closest approach (for hyperbolic paths).
- Eccentricity is indicated visually by how elongated an ellipse appears.

## Additional pedagogical visuals (optional enhancements)

**Animation or interactive element suggestions:**

- **Orbital simulator:** Allow the student to vary the initial velocity and position of a satellite and observe whether it enters a circular orbit, elliptical orbit, parabolic escape, or hyperbolic approach.

- **Torsion balance simulator:** Show how the twist of the fiber changes as the large masses move closer to or farther from the small ones, reinforcing that gravitational force obeys the inverse square law.

- **Kepler's Second Law animation:** Show a planet moving along an ellipse, with the radius vector sweeping out area at a constant rate (areal velocity), and the planet visibly moving faster near perihelion and slower near aphelion.

These are not required for a printed textbook but would enhance a digital version.
