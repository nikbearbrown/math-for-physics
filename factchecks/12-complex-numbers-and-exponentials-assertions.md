## Full Findings

### Historical attributions (web-verified)

1. **Cardano, *Ars Magna* (1545) — cubic/quartic solutions; square roots of negatives "as subtle as they are useless."** CONFIRMED. Standard, well-documented attribution; *Ars Magna* (1545) is Cardano's algebra treatise and the source of the dismissive remark on roots of negatives.

2. **Bombelli, *L'Algebra* (1572) — the cubic x³ = 15x + 4 with imaginary intermediates yielding the real root 4.** CONFIRMED. Bombelli's *L'Algebra* (1572) is the textbook-standard origin of disciplined arithmetic with √−1; the x³ = 15x + 4 example is the canonical one. The on-page arithmetic ((2+i)³ = 2 + 11i) is AI-verified below.

3. **Euler, *Introductio in analysin infinitorum* (1748, E101), Book I — e^{iθ} = cos θ + i sin θ from the power series.** CONFIRMED. Euler derives e^{iv} = cos v + i sin v in Chapter VIII of the *Introductio* (E101, 1748). (Cotes had a logarithmic precursor in 1714, which does not contradict the chapter's attribution of the exponential form to Euler 1748.)
   URL: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Introductio_in_analysin_infinitorum ; http://eulerarchive.maa.org/backup/E101.html

4. **Wessel (1799) and Argand (1806) — geometric (complex-plane) interpretation; legitimacy due to Gauss (1831).** CONFIRMED. MacTutor/Wikipedia: Wessel was first (1799, Copenhagen Academy), Argand rediscovered it independently (1806), and Gauss's 1831 treatise established modern notation/terminology. The chapter's framing — Wessel invented it, named for Argand who rediscovered it, legitimized by Gauss — is accurate.
   URL: https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/Biographies/Wessel/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_number
