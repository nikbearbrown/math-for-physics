# Mathematics for Physics

## Title
Mathematics for Physics — The Tools, Derived on the Page, Motivated by Mechanics

## Author
Nik Bear Brown

## One-sentence pitch
A methods book that teaches the mathematics the introductory physics sequence depends on — dimensional analysis, vectors, calculus, linear systems, differential equations, and Fourier analysis — with every tool derived on the page and motivated by a concrete physics problem, so the reader learns the math *as math* and leaves able to use it anywhere.

## The reorganization
This book was reorganized from a physics-topic sequence (units → kinematics → dynamics → … → sound) into a **math-topic** sequence. The physics is no longer the subject; it is the motivating context and the source of worked examples. Each chapter's subject is a mathematical tool. The physics problem opens the chapter to show why the tool is needed, the math is then developed and derived for its own sake, and the physics returns as application.

The prior physics chapters are retained as source material in `chapters/_physics-source/` (worked examples, derivations, and figures to mine) — they are not part of the book.

## Audience
Engineering, physics, chemistry, and pre-med students taking the calculus-based introductory sequence, often concurrently with Calculus I/II — and self-studiers who want the mathematics behind the physics rather than plug-and-chug. They have high-school physics and some algebra; they are now taking the math seriously and will be tested on derivations. No prior college calculus is assumed; the book builds it where the physics needs it.

## Voice and rules
- Math is the subject; physics is the example. Every chapter title names a mathematical tool, not a physics topic.
- Derive on the page. Where a formula is used, show where it comes from. No memorized results without their origin.
- Every chapter opens with one concrete physics problem that the chapter's math is required to solve, states the tool plainly, develops it, then returns to the problem.
- Notation defined at first use. Calibrated, honest about what is proved vs. asserted.
- Worked examples drawn from the retained physics source chapters (kinematics, work, rotation, oscillation, waves, sound).

## Scope (in)
The mathematical methods of introductory mechanics and waves: dimensional analysis and error; algebra and equation-solving; trigonometry and geometry; vector algebra; functions, graphs, and power laws; limits and differentiation; vector-valued/parametric differentiation; integration and its techniques/applications; linear systems and basic matrices; ordinary differential equations and oscillatory motion; complex numbers and exponentials; series and approximations; partial derivatives, the wave equation, and Fourier analysis.

## Scope (out)
Full multivariable/vector calculus beyond what mechanics needs (divergence/curl theorems), abstract linear algebra, real-analysis rigor, probability/statistics, and physics content for its own sake (no new physics that is not used to motivate a mathematical tool).

## High-level arc
**Part I — Quantities and Geometry:** dimensions, algebra, trigonometry, vectors. (The language.)
**Part II — Calculus:** functions, limits, derivatives, vector-valued derivatives, integrals and their applications. (The engine.)
**Part III — Systems and Change:** linear systems, differential equations, complex exponentials, series, partial derivatives/PDEs, Fourier. (The hard tools.)
